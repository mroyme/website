_Why "lost" was easier to translate from Bengali into English than into Hindi_

## A line in the background

I was not looking for a linguistics problem. I was watching one of those videos that autoplays while you are doing something else, and underneath the voiceover was "Monta Re", from _Lootera_. Amit Trivedi's tune, Amitabh Bhattacharya's words, a Hindi song with a Bengali line stitched into the refrain.

The Bengali line has a word in it: দিশাহারা, _dishahara_. Directionless. At a loss. A heart that cannot find which way is which.

I did not stop the video for that. What stopped me, a minute or two later, was an idle thought: if a Hindi-speaking friend asked me what that line meant, what would I actually say?

I speak both languages. This should have taken four seconds.

The trouble is not _direction_. Hindi has perfectly good words for directionless: दिशाहीन, _dishahin_, or भटका हुआ, _bhatka hua_, wandering, gone astray. Nothing missing there.

The trouble starts when I try to keep the shape of the Bengali. _Dishahara_ is not built from the ordinary Bengali word for "without". Its second half is _hara_, the form that keeps appearing whenever Bengali talks about loss and deprivation. Direction, plus something like _bereft of_.

And the moment I reach for _lost_ in Hindi, everyday Hindi makes me choose.

There is **हारना**, _harna_: to lose in the sense of being beaten. You lose a match, a case, an election.

And there is **खोना**, _khona_: to lose in the sense of no longer having. You lose a key, an opportunity, a person.

_Harna_ is wrong for _dishahara_, at least in ordinary modern Hindi. Nobody has beaten the direction. Direction is not an opponent. _Khona_ is much closer. You can lose a key, lose someone, lose your way. But the construction itself refuses to come across. Nobody says _dishakhoya_. I could explain the meaning in Hindi easily enough. I could not preserve the Bengali shape.

So I did what you do, and reached for the third language.

> _a heart that's lost its way_

That is the sentence I wanted. English **lost** keeps both the meaning and the shape.

Once I noticed that, English _lose_ began to look unusually broad. You can lose a match, lose a war, lose your keys, lose your mother, lose your way, lose your nerve, lose hope, lose your mind, lose yourself. You can simply be lost.

Competitive defeat, misplacement, bereavement, disorientation, and several kinds of inner collapse can all sit under the same verb.

Nor is English alone. German **verlieren** does much the same work: a game, a key, a person, your bearings, your hope, yourself. My French is far weaker, so I checked rather than trusting my ear, but **perdre** has a similar range: _perdre un match_, _perdre ses clefs_, _perdre quelqu'un_, _perdre son chemin_, _se perdre_.

That could have made English, German and French look like the ordinary pattern. Then I remembered Japanese, which after German is the language I have put the most hours into. Japanese does not package this area of meaning into one everyday verb. 負ける, _makeru_, is being defeated. なくす, _nakusu_, is misplacing your umbrella. 失う, _ushinau_, is losing something serious or abstract: hope, trust, a job, a person. Losing your way is 道に迷う, _michi ni mayou_, becoming confused on the road.

So the little puzzle changed shape. A broad word for losing is not the natural arrangement, with Hindi as the awkward exception. Hindi divides the field. Japanese divides it further. English, German and French barely divide it at all.

What stayed odd was that Bengali, the language right next to Hindi in my head, seemed in this one corner to be behaving more like English.

## Not quite one word

The first version of the thought was: Bengali uses the same word for losing a game and losing your mother.

That is snappy. It is also not quite true, and the correction is more interesting than the slogan.

Bengali does not have one word here. It has a small family of related forms, and they are divided up, just not along the same line that Hindi uses.

**হারা**, _hara_, is intransitive: to be beaten, to lose at a game. _Amra here gechhi_: we lost.

**হারানো**, _harano_, is transitive: to lose something. _Chabi harano_, to lose a key. _Sujog harano_, to lose an opportunity. _Kauke harano_, to lose someone.

And _harano_ is also how you defeat somebody. _Dol-ta-ke harano_: to beat that team.

That last one is the detail I keep coming back to. The same transitive form can mean either _lose X_ or _defeat X_, and Bengali speakers never trip over it. You lose keys. You beat teams. Nobody hears _dol-ta-ke harano_ and thinks the team has been misplaced.

Bengali also has খোয়ানো, _khoyano_, which overlaps with Hindi _khona_. So Bengali is not using a tool Hindi lacks. Both languages have more than one way to talk about loss. They simply lean on their options differently.

The better version of the observation is this: Bengali has a broad _har-_ / _hara_ / _harano_ family, spread across defeat, dispossession, deprivation and bereavement, and its territory overlaps English _lose_ more than I expected.

Then _dishahara_ shifts the light again.

It cannot mean "direction defeated". দিশা, _disha_, means direction. The only available reading is "bereft of direction". Once I started looking at _hara_ as the second half of a compound rather than only as a verb, Bengali turned out to be full of it:

| Compound | Reading |
|---|---|
| পথহারা, _path-hara_ | having lost the road |
| মা-হারা, _ma-hara_ | motherless |
| পিতৃহারা, _pitri-hara_ | fatherless |
| গৃহহারা, _griha-hara_ | homeless |
| আত্মহারা, _atma-hara_ | beside oneself, self-lost |
| সর্বহারা, _sarba-hara_ | having lost everything |

Every one of these is _X, deprived of_. Not _X, defeated_. The word that started the question had already suggested the direction of the answer.

Only quietly, though. This is where I had to stop making the story too tidy. All of that tells me how modern Bengali feels and behaves. It does not tell me where the forms came from, or which meaning is older.

Speaking a language fluently tells you what it does. It tells you nothing at all about where it came from.

## Next door

Assamese was the obvious next place to look, not because I had a grand plan, but because it is the other language in this family that I actually speak. When you have a hunch about Bengali, Assamese is sitting right there to try it on.

The answer was yes, mostly. Assamese has _har-_ doing defeat. It has _-hara_ compounds, including দিশাহাৰা, _dishahara_. And it has হেৰোৱা, _herowa_, for losing something, or for being lost, the way a person can be lost in a forest.

So Bengali was not alone. Something similar was happening next door.

Then I looked at the form again. _Herowa_. Not _harowa_.

The vowel bothered me. My first temptation was to connect it to Bengali _here_, as in _amra here gechhi_. Perhaps Bengali kept both _a_ and _e_ forms, while Assamese carried one further. It made a neat story, which is not the same as making it true.

So I leave it smaller than I first wanted to. Modern Bengali and Assamese both let this family of forms cover defeat, deprivation, dispossession and being lost. That does not make it a grand eastern theory. It just means the pattern was not a private Bengali oddity.

For this essay, that is enough.

## A little history

My first real theory was tidy.

Bengali, I thought, had held on to a broad old word for LOSS, while Hindi had divided the territory in two, giving defeat to _harna_ and dispossession to _khona_. Bengali the conservative, Hindi the innovator. English, from a different lexical line, had somehow arrived at a similar range.

The history made that story less tidy, which is usually what history is good for.

The trail leads back to an old Sanskrit family of words around taking, carrying off, depriving and seizing. One related form, _harayati_, is listed in dictionaries with meanings around having something taken away, being deprived, losing something, and losing at play.

I will not pretend to reconstruct the whole path from Sanskrit to Bengali and Hindi. But even this small glimpse changes the mood of the question. Defeat and deprivation were not always far apart. In a game with stakes, losing the game and having something taken from you are not two wholly separate events. They are one event seen from two angles.

That makes modern Hindi more interesting than I first gave it credit for. Its inherited **harna** now mostly belongs to defeat, while **khona** carries much of ordinary dispossession. But older breadth remains in familiar phrases: _himmat harna_, to lose courage, and _dhairya harna_, to lose patience. Nobody has beaten you in either case.

Those phrases were sitting in plain sight in a language I speak. I had walked past them because I thought I already knew what _harna_ meant.

## Back to the song

At this point it would be tempting to drag English into the same family story. Bengali and English are both Indo-European, after all. But _hara_ and _lose_ are not cognates. They do not come from the same ancestral word.

English _lose_ comes down its own Germanic line. French _perdre_ comes from another lexical line again. Yet English, French and German all allow one everyday verb to range across defeat, misplacement, bereavement, disorientation and abstraction in ways that feel strangely familiar from Bengali.

That is the part I like most. There does not need to be a deep connection underneath for the resemblance to be interesting.

Languages can arrive at similar maps from different roads. Close relatives can redraw an inherited map differently. Unrelated words can end up occupying similar territory by coincidence, metaphor, habit, or all the ordinary mess through which languages change.

The useful word here is lexicalisation: the way languages package meaning into words. One language bundles defeat, bereavement, misplacement and disorientation together. Another splits them between two common verbs. Another splits them further. None of them is more logical. None of them is more poetic by default. They simply carve the space differently.

You usually do not notice the carving. You notice it when you try to translate a sentence that should have taken four seconds.

So where does that leave _Monta Re_?

If I were translating _dishahara_ into Hindi, I would probably give up on preserving the compound and write something natural: a heart gone astray, a heart that has lost its way, a heart without direction. Hindi can say the thought perfectly well. It just asks me to choose a different shape.

English, by chance, lets me keep both the meaning and the shape:

> _a heart that's lost its way_

That does not mean Bengali is secretly closer to English than to Hindi. It does not mean Hindi is missing something. It means that in this one small corner of vocabulary, Bengali and English let a word family stretch across a similar range, while Hindi now draws a firmer line between being beaten and losing possession.

A word can be lost. A way can be lost. A match, a mother, a key, a country, a nerve, a self. Some languages keep those losses apart. Some let one word cover more of them.

I started with a line in the background of a video I was barely watching. I ended up with a smaller, useful reminder: fluency has limits, translation is not substitution, and two neighbouring languages do not always meet each other directly.
